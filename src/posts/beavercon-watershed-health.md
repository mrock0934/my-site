date: 2026-10-01
summary: Notes from BeaverCon 2026 on building a satellite-backed health record for every watershed, starting in Wisconsin.

It was a pleasure to make my way to Minneapolis for BeaverCon 2026. It's remarkable to see how much has changed since the first one in 2020, with great energy and people from all over the world. Remote sensing ran through many of the sessions, with aerial, drone, and satellite work all being used to monitor the ways beavers reshape a landscape.

I was there with the Superior Bio-Conservancy. We have seen how we can influence beaver management policy in states like Colorado and California, and the story of a keystone species continues to carry real weight with decision makers. As an advocate for open data for good, I see Mergin Maps and QGIS as the tools to start building a data library for each watershed across the Laurentian Mixed Forest Province. Through university hubs, we can facilitate community-driven data collection on beaver populations in Wisconsin, where the DNR hasn't conducted a proper assessment in over a decade. Without one, we can't understand the impact of trapping on beavers or the effects on the large predators that depend on them. The public trust doctrine gives this real weight: Wisconsin holds its wildlife in trust for all residents, and a trustee has a duty to know the condition of what it manages. A public repository of population data, managed by public institutions and starting in Wisconsin, helps the state meet that duty.

High resolution satellite data is the most powerful analytical tool we can put behind this effort. It lets us identify riparian corridors and map the vegetation, stream gradients, and floodplain conditions that make a stretch of river suitable for beavers, then track how ponds, wetlands, and riparian zones change across whole watersheds over time. Field collection confirms what the imagery shows. Universities are well positioned to run this analysis, and as more hubs adopt the approach, they strengthen the conservation market for remotely sensed data. The volume of work is enormous: the United States alone contains on the order of two thousand HUC8 subbasins, and each one needs repeat imagery, elevation, and spectral coverage to build and maintain a health record. Local metropolitan sewer districts and other public water stakeholders can fund the hubs to deliver that record, and the hubs in turn become a steady, repeatable source of demand for commercial satellite providers. Volume also brings costs down. Hubs that coordinate their purchases, through shared data agreements or a consortium buying across many watersheds at once, can negotiate lower per-watershed pricing, and a single archive of imagery, elevation, and trained models can serve every partner in a region. For providers, that means reliable conservation customers across thousands of watersheds, and for universities and their public funders, it means each additional watershed costs less to add than the one before.

Stereoscopic satellite imagery takes this a step further. Overlapping high resolution images captured from different angles can be processed into three-dimensional surface models, giving us elevation across entire watersheds without waiting on an airborne campaign. In pond modeling, those surfaces let us estimate the extent, depth, and storage volume of existing beaver ponds, and compare them over time to see how much water a complex holds and how that changes by season and year. That is the same attenuation function MMSD funded in Milwaukee, now measurable at the scale of a whole watershed.

The same elevation data can look backward. Before settlement, the Western Great Lakes held some of the highest beaver densities in North America, and SBC's work describes how beaver dam complexes formed stair-step gradients along rivers and streams. Relict dams and filled ponds leave subtle signatures in the terrain: flat valley-floor benches, abrupt breaks in stream gradient, and sediment-filled basins behind old impoundments. Fine-scale elevation models can surface those features across thousands of stream miles, pointing us to places where beaver once shaped the hydrology and where recovery has the strongest foundation.

Three-dimensional satellite-based lidar models strengthen this analysis considerably. Models trained on lidar-derived terrain and canopy height can learn to estimate bare-earth elevation and vegetation structure from satellite imagery, extending lidar-quality information to places where airborne coverage is old, sparse, or absent. Spaceborne lidar adds calibration points, and Wisconsin's existing airborne lidar provides strong training data to start with. Because those models can be trained once and applied across many watersheds, they fit the university hub structure well: a hub in one region can share trained models and methods with the next, and each new watershed makes the shared toolset better.

Hyperspectral sensors add the layer of speciation. Where the 3D approach tells us the shape of the land and the water, hyperspectral data identifies what is growing on it, separating preferred beaver forage such as aspen and willow from conifer and other cover. That sharpens habitat suitability well beyond land cover classes, and it lets us flag the stretches of stream where food, terrain, and hydrology line up. Down the road, the same combination of elevation, water extent, and vegetation chemistry can feed models of carbon flux and natural methane emissions across the region. Beaver wetlands store carbon and also emit methane, and measuring both at watershed scale will give us a far clearer picture of their net role in the regional carbon balance.

Optical imagery, much of it already open source, completes the picture by showing how the system moves. Repeat coverage reveals the dynamic expansion and recession of riparian areas through seasons and drought cycles, and shows where beaver wetlands persist when the surrounding landscape dries. That same record supports wildfire work. Normalized burn indices such as dNBR map burn severity after a fire, and comparing those maps against the wet corridors beavers create shows where ponds and riparian zones act as natural firebreaks and refugia. With open Landsat and Sentinel-2 data, universities can identify where recovering beaver populations would add the most protection, which turns wildfire prevention into another reason to restore the species.

Satellite data also sets the order of operations for everything closer to the ground. Wide-area monitoring acts as the tip: it flags a new pond, a shift in riparian extent, a stretch of suitable habitat, or a candidate relict dam. That tip cues drones and aircraft to collect higher resolution imagery, lidar, or hyperspectral data over those locations, and the insights from above direct ground crews to the sites that matter most. Field teams arrive knowing what they are looking for and where, so each trip confirms and refines the model, and the limited time of students, volunteers, and agency staff goes to the highest-value locations. This tiered approach, from satellite to aerial to ground, raises the quality of the data at each step while keeping costs manageable, and Mergin Maps closes the loop by returning field observations to the hub's health record.

Those partnerships with metropolitan sewer districts bring internal stakeholders who need the data to be good. In the Milwaukee River watershed, MMSD funded a study with Milwaukee Riverkeeper and UW-Milwaukee that estimated the river could support around 840 beaver colonies, with dams slowing peak flows before they reach the city. With that backing, each watershed will have a health record managed by the university hub in that region. Beaver Recovery Zones, like the 25-year zone SBC is pursuing in the Milwaukee River Watershed, will be the priority areas where this work begins before it expands to the rest of each watershed.

Tribal partnership is central to all of it. The Lake Superior watershed lies within the Ceded Territories, and we aim to deepen our existing collaboration with tribes across the region, including GLIFWC corridor mapping that brings Traditional Ecological Knowledge into the picture.

The Milwaukee River watershed is where the model starts. MMSD, UW-Milwaukee, and Milwaukee Riverkeeper have already shown how a sewer district and a university can work together, and that framework gives other university hubs a template. Each hub will prioritize the watersheds in its region that can adopt the same structure, and from there the network grows across Wisconsin, then outward into an interstate and multinational effort spanning Minnesota, Michigan, and Ontario. That expansion brings administrative boundaries and ecological boundaries into focus. Management, data collection, and funding follow state and national lines, while beavers, wolves, and water follow watersheds. A health record organized around the watershed gives agencies, universities, and tribal nations in different jurisdictions a shared picture of the landscape.

This begins as a microscale project and carries the potential to become a generational one. The Lake Superior watershed is the right first leap. It is the wellhead for major river systems and the most pristine of the Great Lakes, and it holds a conservation success story that deserves far more attention. Wolves in the Western Great Lakes recovered from fewer than 200 in 1973 to over 4,000 in 2019, a story often muted by the attention given to Yellowstone. That recovery shows the landscape is intact enough to support full restoration, and beavers, the other keystone species of this region, are the natural next chapter. Every layer described here, from elevation and speciation to carbon, fire, and water, is centered on those two keystone species and the landscapes they shape. The result is a full health record for the watershed today and, over time, the evidence base for any prescriptions it needs in the future. That framework can then be replicated across the Laurentian Forest Province and around the world as a way to better understand our watersheds.
